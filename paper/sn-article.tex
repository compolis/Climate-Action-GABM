%%==================== ABSTRACT ====================%%
\abstract{%
Small political groups can shape public debate far beyond their size,
but models of this process often strip away the people doing the
persuading. This paper presents a generative agent-based model (GABM)
of UK climate-policy opinion designed for that problem. Each citizen
agent is anchored to a real respondent in a representative YouGov
survey panel, so the simulation starts from observed opinions rather
than opinions generated from scratch by a language model. Two political
agents, loosely modelled on Reform UK and the Green Party, send competing
messages, and the framework lets the researcher vary who is reached and
how messages are delivered. We report two illustrative probes: a
seven-day run under symmetric political broadcasts, and a four-day
single-policy run that varies broadcast reach. We also test whether the
survey component reads the personas it is given and document where
residual bias remains. This is a short methodological note, not a
tipping claim. Its purpose is to present the framework, report the
first results, and identify the main questions for the next round of
work.
}

\keywords{generative agent-based modelling, opinion dynamics, committed minorities, large language models, climate policy, methodology}

%% 1. INTRODUCTION

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Public opinion on contested policy is rarely uniform, and it is rarely
static. Small, organised groups can shape debate well beyond their own
size. In this paper, we use the term \emph{committed minorities} for
political actors that hold a stable position and keep pressing the same
message over time. UK climate politics offers a clear example. Reform
UK and the Green Party of England and Wales hold only a small share of
seats in Parliament, but their arguments about carbon taxes, petrol
cars, renewable energy, and home heating travel well beyond their own
voters. A basic question follows: how do such groups shift wider public
opinion, and under what conditions does one side gain ground?

Classical models of committed-minority dynamics offer a useful starting
point because they show how a small group can sometimes move a much
larger population~\cite{centola2018, granovetter1978, galam2002}. But
they usually simplify citizens into binary opinion states and fixed
update rules. Generative agent-based models (GABMs) take a different
approach. In a GABM, agents use a language model to generate messages,
reflections, and survey answers~\cite{park2023, tornberg2023,
ghaffarzadegan2024, vezhnevets2023concordia}. This makes it possible to
represent richer personas and arguments, but it also introduces a known
problem. When language models are asked for opinions from scratch, they
often lean toward socially favoured positions and compress variation
across respondents~\cite{argyle2023, santurkar2023, bisbee2024,
manivannan2026}. For climate policy, that can make a simulation start
from unrealistically pro-climate views unless the starting point is
carefully controlled.

This paper presents a GABM of UK climate-policy opinion designed around
that problem. Each citizen agent is anchored to a real respondent in a
representative YouGov survey panel. Two political agents, loosely
modelled on Reform UK and the Green Party, act as competing committed
minorities and send opposing messages. The framework separates several
features that a researcher may want to vary, including who is reached,
what message is sent, and which model produces it. The main
contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a citizen-agent design in which persona, prior vote choice, and
  Day~0 opinions are seeded from a real survey respondent rather than
  generated from scratch by a language model;
  \item a two-political-agent architecture in which broadcast reach,
  message style, and persuasive capability can be varied separately;
  \item a two-part strategy for controlling known language-model bias:
  a Day~0 anchor to the survey ground truth, and a debiased end-of-day
  survey prompt;
  \item two illustrative probes, plus an evaluation exercise, that show
  what the framework can already do and where its main limits remain.
\end{enumerate}

The broader motivation is to understand how generative AI might help us
study public responses to climate policy before policy is implemented,
an agenda we have outlined elsewhere~\cite{manivannan2026}. This paper,
however, is narrower in scope. It is a short methodological report for
seminar discussion and feedback. It does not try to identify tipping
points. Instead, it asks a simpler question: can this framework run
from start to finish, produce interpretable trajectories, and register
a directional response when one key design choice is changed? The
remainder of the paper follows that logic. Section~\ref{sec:model}
describes the framework. Section~\ref{sec:levers} sets out the main design
choices and levers. Section~\ref{sec:probes} reports the two
illustrative probes. Section~\ref{sec:grounding} reports the evaluation
exercise. Sections~\ref{sec:limits} and~\ref{sec:roadmap} set out the
current limits of the framework and the feedback we seek.

%% 2. FRAMEWORK
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Framework}\label{sec:model}

This section gives the parts of the framework that matter for reading
the results. The focus is on four questions: who the agents are, how
messages move through the population, how a simulated day unfolds, and
how starting opinions and daily survey readouts are controlled.

\subsection{Citizen agents and personas}\label{subsec:citizens}

The citizen population is drawn from a representative YouGov UK panel
of about 1{,}500 respondents commissioned by the University of Leeds and
fielded in late April 2024. Each citizen agent is tied to one respondent record. That
record contributes demographic background, political history, and a set
of value and ideology measures, which are converted into a short
first-person \emph{persona}: a natural-language description that the
language model treats as the agent's identity throughout a run.

The same respondent record also provides the agent's observed answers on
the six climate-policy items. Those answers are collected on the
original A--G survey scale and mapped to a $-3$ to $+3$ numeric scale,
where negative values indicate opposition, zero indicates a neutral
position, and positive values indicate support. These observed answers
serve both as the Day~0 anchor (Section~\ref{subsec:anchor}) and as the
ground truth for the evaluation exercise in Section~\ref{sec:grounding}.

\subsection{Competing political agents}\label{subsec:political}

Two political agents act as fixed elite voices in the model: a
pro-climate agent loosely modelled on the Green Party of England and
Wales, and an anti-climate agent loosely modelled on Reform UK. They do
not update their positions during a run, so they function as the model's
committed minorities.

Each political agent has three separable settings: the stance it
advocates, the audience it can reach, and the prompt and model
configuration used to write its broadcasts. That separation matters
because later experiments can vary reach without changing message style,
or vary message style without changing audience.

\subsection{Exposure structure}\label{subsec:network}

Peer exchange and political exposure are handled separately. Citizens
are linked to one another through a stochastic block model with denser
ties within broad left and right voting blocs than across them
($p_{\mathrm{intra}}=0.15$, $p_{\mathrm{inter}}=0.02$). Political
exposure is assigned from vote history rather than from the peer graph:
citizens with Conservative or Reform histories, or a Leave vote, are
routed toward the anti-climate broadcasts; citizens aligned with
stronger climate action are routed toward the pro-climate broadcasts;
and a configurable share can be exposed to both. Keeping these two
structures separate lets the framework vary peer contact and political
reach independently.

\subsection{Daily phase loop}\label{subsec:phases}

Each simulated day has three message phases and one survey readout. By
default, the pro-climate agent broadcasts to its audience, exposed
citizens write a short private reflection, the anti-climate agent does
the same, and citizens then exchange peer messages with $k$ randomly
chosen neighbours before writing another brief reflection. The ordering
of those phases can itself be changed as an experimental condition.

At the end of the day, each citizen is asked the original survey
questions again and the answers are recorded on the same $-3$ to $+3$
scale. The framework can run this loop in \emph{per-policy} mode, where
one policy is studied in isolation, or in \emph{package} mode, where
the six-policy bundle is discussed together.

\subsection{Day~0 anchoring}\label{subsec:anchor}

The main control on initial conditions is how Day~0 is set. In
\emph{LLM-survey} mode, the model answers the survey cold and those
answers become the starting point. This reproduces the baseline-bias
problem documented in prior work~\cite{argyle2023, santurkar2023,
bisbee2024, manivannan2026} and is kept mainly as a control. In
\emph{ground-truth} mode, the agent starts from the respondent's
observed survey answers. In \emph{ground-truth-with-rationale} mode,
which is used in the reported runs, the agent also generates a short
first-person explanation for those seeded answers.

This anchored mode gives the agent a narrative starting point without
showing it a running numeric history of its own opinions. At the cohort
level it has a simple consequence: the Day~0 mean equals the YouGov mean
by construction, so any later movement is attributable to the simulated
interactions rather than to the language model's prior over climate
policy.

\subsection{Memory and end-of-day survey readout}\label{subsec:debias}

Each agent carries a tiered memory: the current day's reflections are
available in full, the previous two days are kept verbatim, and older
material is compressed into short daily summaries. We deliberately do
not show the model a running table of its own past numeric scores.
Earlier versions did this, and the result was too much self-consistency:
agents tended to repeat their previous numbers rather than respond to
the day's messages.

End-of-day surveys use a two-step debiasing prompt. First, the agent is
asked under an anti-sycophancy instruction to explain its current view
from the persona's standpoint. In a second call, it converts that view
into an A--G survey response. This is the survey path used in the
results and evaluation sections. A third-person wording of the question
performed worse in our prompt comparisons and was dropped; extended
thinking remains optional where the underlying model supports it.

%% 3. EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN AND MANIPULATIONS
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Experimental design and manipulations}\label{sec:levers}

The framework is meant to support controlled experiments rather than
one fixed simulation recipe. In principle, opinion change can depend on
who receives political messages, what those messages say, how citizens
are connected, and how long the interaction lasts. For the present
paper, the key point is narrower: most of these choices are held fixed,
and one is deliberately changed so we can see whether the model
registers a directional response.

\subsection{Exposure and network choices}\label{subsec:levers-structural}

The main structural manipulation is broadcast reach. Each political
agent has its own reach parameter in $[0,1]$, so the model can compare
symmetric exposure with asymmetric exposure favouring one side. This is
the manipulation used in the reach-asymmetry probe in
Section~\ref{subsec:probe-reach}.

Audience assignment is currently based on vote history, as described in
Section~\ref{subsec:network}, and peer exchange is governed by the
block-model network plus peer fan-out $k$. These settings matter
substantively, but they are not the main object of comparison in the
present paper. They define the contact environment within which the
reach manipulation operates.

\subsection{Message and schedule choices}\label{subsec:levers-message}

The content of political messaging can also be varied independently of
reach. Each political agent can use a different prompt, rhetorical
style, or underlying language model. That makes it possible to study
persuasive capability or framing effects, including moral reframing of
the kind studied by~\cite{feinbergwiller2015}, without changing who is
exposed.

The day schedule is another design choice. One can alter how often each
side broadcasts, whether peer exchange is present, and the order of the
within-day phases. In the current paper these choices are mostly held
fixed so that the reported probes remain interpretable. Probe~1 uses
the full daily loop; Probe~2 switches peer exchange off to keep the
structural comparison simple, but the result of interest is still the
ordering induced by reach asymmetry.

\subsection{Scale, scope, and what is left for later}\label{subsec:levers-population}

Population size $N$, simulation horizon $T$, and policy scope determine
what kind of claim a run can support. The framework can run one policy
at a time or the full six-policy package, and it can be applied to
subgroups of the YouGov panel rather than only to mixed cohorts. It can
also, in principle, vary the effective size of the competing minorities
by changing the sample or reweighting the panel, which is important for
the tipping-style questions that motivate this broader research
programme~\cite{centola2018}.

These choices matter because they separate a proof of operation from a
substantive test. The runs in Section~\ref{sec:probes} use small
cohorts and short horizons, which are enough to show that the framework
runs end to end and that one manipulation registers a signal, but not
enough to support strong claims about long-run dynamics or tipping.
Across the reported runs, the only lever treated as a substantive
experimental manipulation is broadcast reach asymmetry. The rest of the
design space defines the next set of experiments rather than claims
made here.

\section{Results}\label{sec:probes}

This section reports two illustrative probes. Probe~1 asks whether the
full framework runs end to end under symmetric political broadcasts and
produces readable trajectories. Probe~2 then varies one design choice,
broadcast reach, to test whether the cohort moves in the expected
direction. Neither probe is intended as an effect-size estimate or a
tipping test.

\subsection{Probe~1: end-to-end run under symmetric broadcasts}\label{subsec:probe-package}

This probe asks whether the framework produces stable, interpretable
trajectories when both political agents broadcast at full reach. The
run uses $N=30$ citizen agents drawn from the YouGov panel, seven
simulated days, the full six-policy package, peer messaging on, and
ground-truth-with-rationale anchoring at Day~0. Reflections, peer
messages, and political broadcasts are generated by GPT-5-mini at
temperature $0.5$; the end-of-day survey is run separately through
Claude Sonnet under the two-step debiasing protocol in
Section~\ref{subsec:debias}. The aim is to establish a readable
baseline before turning to the asymmetric comparison in Probe~2.

\paragraph{Main result.} At the package level, the run is stable rather
than explosive (Figures~\ref{fig:probe1-package-shares}
and~\ref{fig:probe1-package-index}). The supporting share starts at
$73\%$ on Day~0 and remains in the $73$--$80\%$ range; the against
share rises from $10\%$ to roughly $13$--$23\%$; and the neutral band,
which is $17\%$ on Day~0, is largely absorbed by Day~2. The mean
package index begins at the anchored $+0.96$ and settles into a narrow
plateau between $+1.16$ and $+1.22$ from Day~1 onward. The cohort does
not collapse to the centre or cascade to an extreme, and the individual
trajectories remain spread across the full $-3$ to $+3$ scale.

\paragraph{Policy detail.} The aggregate pattern hides clear
policy-level differences (see Figs.~S1 and~S2 in the SI). Renewable
energy is already near a
ceiling, with support rising from $80\%$ to $93\%$ by Day~1 and a mean
near $+2$ throughout. The largest upward shifts are on Carbon tax and
Climate compensation: Carbon-tax support moves from $53\%$ to $77\%$
on Day~1 and settles around $80\%$, with the mean rising from $+0.77$
to $+1.20$ by Day~7; Climate-compensation support rises from $40\%$ to
about $65\%$, with the mean rising from $+0.10$ to about $+0.65$.
Green housing is the one case with a mild mean decline, from $+1.53$
to $+1.33$, even though support stays in the $80$--$87\%$ range. Ban
petrol cars moves differently again: support rises from $50\%$ to
$60$--$67\%$, against rises from $27\%$ to $33$--$40\%$, the neutral
category disappears by Day~2, and the mean shifts only into a narrow
$+0.60$--$+0.77$ band. In other words, shares and means do not always
tell the same story.

\paragraph{What it shows.} Probe~1 shows that the framework runs from
start to finish, holds the Day~0 anchor, and produces trajectories that
are readable at both the package and policy level. It does not identify
effect sizes, robustness across seeds or models, or any long-horizon
dynamic.

\subsection{Probe~2: reach asymmetry registers a signal}\label{subsec:probe-reach}

This probe asks whether changing one design choice in isolation produces
a directional response. We restrict attention to one policy, Ban petrol
cars, shorten the schedule to four days, switch peer messaging off, and
hold all other settings fixed at the same Day~0 anchor, network,
prompts, and model stack as in Probe~1. To keep reach as the only
active manipulation, each political agent's daily audience is capped at
$20$ eligible citizens drawn uniformly at random, after which a reach
probability is applied. The three conditions are symmetric
($p_R = p_G = 1.0$), Reform-dominant ($p_R = 1.0$, $p_G = 0.25$), and
Green-dominant ($p_R = 0.25$, $p_G = 1.0$). If the lever matters, the
Day-4 cohort mean should order Reform-dominant $<$ symmetric
$<$ Green-dominant on a pro-climate scale.

\paragraph{Main result.} Across three independently sampled
$N=30$ cohorts, the Day-4 mean opinion orders Reform-dominant
$<$ symmetric $<$ Green-dominant in all three replications. The
ordering appears by Day~2 in all three replications, and by Day~1 in
two of them. The Day-4 gap between Green-dominant and Reform-dominant
ranges from $+0.17$ to $+0.80$ opinion units on the $-3$ to $+3$
scale, with a mean across the three replications of about $+0.58$. The
sign of the effect is therefore robust, but its magnitude is clearly
cohort-sensitive.

\paragraph{Illustrative run.} Figures~\ref{fig:probe2-means}
and~\ref{fig:probe2-shares} show one near-neutral replication as a
worked example. In that replication, the Day-4 means order
Reform-dominant ($-0.10$), symmetric ($+0.23$), and Green-dominant
($+0.67$), while the supporting share orders $53\%$, $60\%$, and
$70\%$ respectively. We illustrate that case because it preserves the
same monotone ordering while showing a clearer share-level contrast
than the weakest replication; the strongest replication, by contrast,
shows a larger mean separation but a tie between the symmetric and
Green-dominant conditions on support share.

\paragraph{What it shows.} Probe~2 now supports a stronger but still
narrow methodological claim: when broadcast reach is varied in
isolation, the framework moves in the expected direction across
multiple small cohorts. What it still does not provide is a stable
effect-size estimate, or evidence that the same magnitude would persist
across larger samples, other policies, models, or schedules.

\subsection{What these results show}\label{subsec:probe-interpretation}

Taken together, the two probes support a methodological claim, not a
substantive one. The framework runs end to end under the current model
stack, stays anchored to a sensible empirical starting point, and
registers a directional response when broadcast reach is varied. Probe~2
now shows that the sign of that response survives across three small
independent cohorts, but it still does not pin down a stable effect size or
establish robustness across larger populations, language models,
schedules, or policies, and it does not address tipping. The next
section asks a different prerequisite question: whether the survey path
reads the personas it is given and where residual bias remains.

%% 5. EVALUATION: PERSONA SIGNAL AND RESIDUAL BIAS
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Evaluation: persona signal and residual bias}\label{sec:grounding}

The simulation is anchored to the real YouGov answers at Day~0, but
from Day~1 onward it still depends on the language model's survey path.
That raises two questions: does the survey path actually condition on
the persona it is given, and where does it still lean systematically
in one direction? We answer both on the survey stack used in
Section~\ref{sec:probes} by running it in isolation against the YouGov
ground truth, with no memory, broadcasts, peer exchange, or stored
opinion history between calls. A permutation test (shuffle the model's
answers across personas many times and ask whether the real pairing
fits better than random reassignment) clears comfortably on five of
the six policies in the main $n=30$ cohort, with Renewable energy the
only borderline case. A higher-power $n=100$ follow-up on Carbon tax
and Climate compensation, the two least clear policies in the small
sample, confirms persona signal on both ($p<0.02$ on ordinal accuracy
and $p<10^{-3}$ on MAE). Rank correlations are positive on every
policy. Full protocol, metrics, per-policy tables, and the shuffle-test
distributions are reported in SI~\S S4 and Figs.~S3 and~S4.

Reading persona is not the same as being unbiased. Figure~\ref{fig:calib-bias}
shows a clear split. On Renewable energy, Ban fossil fuels, Ban petrol
cars, and Green housing, residual bias is small, ranging from $+0.07$
to $+0.47$ on the $-3$ to $+3$ scale. On Carbon tax and Climate
compensation, it remains clearly positive: $+0.87$ and $+0.60$ in the
$n=30$ sample, confirmed at $+0.85$ and $+0.48$ in the $n=100$
follow-up. This matters in three ways for the rest of the paper. Day~0
remains clean by anchoring to the observed survey rather than a cold
LLM answer. Probe~2 is tested on Ban petrol cars, which sits in the
low-bias group, so its directional ordering cannot be explained by
survey bias. Probe~1 should be read more cautiously on Carbon tax and
Climate compensation, where upward movement overlaps with a known
pro-climate measurement drift; within-policy change is still
interpretable because each day is measured with the same instrument.
Overall, the survey path reads persona, behaves reasonably on four of
the six policies, and remains too pro-climate on the two cost-framed
ones.

%% 6. SCOPE AND LIMITATIONS
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Scope and limitations}\label{sec:limits}

This paper should be read as a proof of operation for one framework
configuration. It is not a population estimate of UK opinion change,
and it is not a tipping claim. The main constraints fall into three
groups: scope, validity risks already addressed, and validity risks
still open.

\subsection{Current scope}\label{subsec:limits-scope}

The reported runs are deliberately small. Probe~1 uses $N=30$ citizens
over seven days under one random seed, and Probe~2 uses $N=30$ over
four days in three reach conditions, with the reported ordering checked
across three independently sampled cohorts and the figures showing one
of them. Wall time is still dominated by sequential language-model calls, so
larger cohorts and longer horizons were out of scope here. The paper
also uses one model stack per role (GPT-5-mini for reflections, peer
messages, and political broadcasts; Claude Sonnet for end-of-day
surveys), one policy domain, and a two-political-agent abstraction.
Those choices are enough to test whether the framework runs end to end
and whether one manipulation registers a signal. They are not enough to
support population-level claims, cross-model claims, or richer
multi-actor dynamics.

\subsection{Addressed and open validity risks}\label{subsec:limits-validity}

Two important risks are already handled directly. First,
initialisation bias is controlled by the Day~0 anchor in
\S\ref{subsec:anchor}: the cohort starts from observed YouGov answers
rather than opinions generated from scratch by a language model.
Second, survey-path bias is measured rather than assumed away:
Section~\ref{sec:grounding} shows that the evaluation path reads
persona, behaves reasonably on four of the six policies, and retains a
known positive bias on Carbon tax and Climate compensation.

Two further risks remain open. We do not yet know whether opinion
change under peer messaging matches how people respond to peer
influence outside the model; we only know that peer exchange produces
interpretable trajectories in the current runs. We also do not yet know
how much the tiered memory architecture distorts late-run dynamics over
longer horizons, because earlier days are compressed into summaries
rather than preserved in full. These are measurement targets for the
next phase, not background caveats.

\subsection{What would be needed for a tipping claim}\label{subsec:limits-tipping}

The current paper does not test for tipping. Here, tipping would mean a
threshold-like cascade in which a small change in exposure or alignment
produces self-reinforcing population change. Detecting that kind of
behaviour would require longer horizons, replicated seeds, sweeps over
committed-minority size, and an explicit rule for identifying a tipping
event under noise. None of that evidence is presented here. The point
of the next stage is to test systematically whether this framework can
produce that kind of behaviour, and under what conditions.

%% 7. FUTURE WORK AND OPEN QUESTIONS
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Future work and open questions}\label{sec:roadmap}

The framework is now far enough along to support a more structured next
phase. The most useful next steps fall into three groups: robustness
and scale, mechanism tests, and longer-term development.

\subsection{Robustness and scale}\label{subsec:roadmap-probes}

The immediate priority is extension beyond the current three-replication
check. Probe~2 should next be run on at least one additional policy and
across a larger set of replications, so that the reach-asymmetry ordering can be
treated as a robust property of the framework rather than of one short
schedule. The next scaling step is to extend horizon and population size once
intra-phase parallelisation reduces wall time per simulated day. Those
two changes are the minimum required before stronger claims about
stability, rebound, or cascade-like behaviour become credible.

\subsection{Mechanism tests enabled by the current design}\label{subsec:roadmap-mechanisms}

The current framework already supports a broader mechanism programme
than this paper uses. The most direct next manipulations are
committed-minority size, persuasive-capability asymmetry, phase
ordering, peer fan-out, and more or less cross-cut exposure in the
network. It can also support content-focused tests, such as message
framing, single-policy versus package-level runs, and subgroup-specific
responses across different cohort compositions. Taken together, these
levers move the framework from proof of operation toward controlled
comparisons about which features of communication actually drive
change.

\subsection{Longer-term development and useful input}\label{subsec:roadmap-feedback}

Longer-term goals are larger populations, longer horizons, richer elite
competition, and stronger checks on memory and peer influence. Plausible
extensions include more than two political agents, explicit media or
platform mediation, dynamic network rewiring, and cross-model
comparison of the survey path. The most useful suggestions at this
stage are pragmatic ones: which robustness check should come first,
which existing lever is most likely to change the conclusions, and what
empirical comparison would most increase confidence before stronger
claims about cascade dynamics or generalisation are made.

\section{Methods}\label{sec11}

Topical subheadings are allowed. Authors must ensure that their Methods section includes adequate experimental and characterization data necessary for others in the field to reproduce their work. Authors are encouraged to include RIIDs where appropriate. 

\textbf{Ethical approval declarations} (only required where applicable) Any article reporting experiment/s carried out on (i)~live vertebrate (or higher invertebrates), (ii)~humans or (iii)~human samples must include an unambiguous statement within the methods section that meets the following requirements: 

\begin{enumerate}[1.]
\item Approval: a statement which confirms that all experimental protocols were approved by a named institutional and/or licensing committee. Please identify the approving body in the methods section

\item Accordance: a statement explicitly saying that the methods were carried out in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations

\item Informed consent (for experiments involving humans or human tissue samples): include a statement confirming that informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal guardian/s
\end{enumerate}

If your manuscript includes potentially identifying patient/participant information, or if it describes human transplantation research, or if it reports results of a clinical trial then  additional information will be required. Please visit (\url{https://www.nature.com/nature-research/editorial-policies}) for Nature Portfolio journals, (\url{https://www.springer.com/gp/authors-editors/journal-author/journal-author-helpdesk/publishing-ethics/14214}) for Springer Nature journals, or (\url{https://www.biomedcentral.com/getpublished/editorial-policies\#ethics+and+consent}) for BMC.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec12}

Discussions should be brief and focused. In some disciplines use of Discussion or `Conclusion' is interchangeable. It is not mandatory to use both. Some journals prefer a section `Results and Discussion' followed by a section `Conclusion'. Please refer to Journal-level guidance for any specific requirements. 

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec13}

Conclusions may be used to restate your hypothesis or research question, restate your major findings, explain the relevance and the added value of your work, highlight any limitations of your study, describe future directions for research and recommendations. 

In some disciplines use of Discussion or 'Conclusion' is interchangeable. It is not mandatory to use both. Please refer to Journal-level guidance for any specific requirements.
